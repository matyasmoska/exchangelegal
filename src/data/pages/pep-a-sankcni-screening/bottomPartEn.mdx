## **Why must an exchange office screen for PEPs and sanctions?**

A bureau-de-change operator is an obliged entity under the AML Act. Customer identification and due diligence include the duty to establish whether the customer (or its beneficial owner) is a **politically exposed person (PEP)** and whether **international sanctions** apply to them under Act No. 69/2006 Coll., on the Implementation of International Sanctions, and directly applicable EU regulations.

This duty is no formality: **a transaction must not be executed if doing so would breach a binding restrictive measure**. You first have to verify whether the match is genuine and establish the content of the applicable measure – the correct course depends on the particular sanctions regime and may involve a transfer ban, an asset freeze or a reporting duty. A missing screening process is also a typical finding in CNB and FAÚ inspections.

## **Who is a politically exposed person (PEP)?**

Under Section 4(5) of the AML Act, a PEP is a natural person who holds or has held a **prominent public function** (e.g. head of state, minister, member of parliament, judge of a supreme court, ambassador, high-ranking officer, member of a state enterprise body), as well as **close relatives** and **known business associates** of such a person. PEPs are subject to an enhanced regime – among other things, the **origin of the assets and funds** used in the transaction must be established.

## **What are international sanctions and which lists must be checked?**

International sanctions are restrictive measures adopted in particular by the **UN and the European Union** (and implemented in Czech law), typically asset freezes and the prohibition of making funds available to designated persons. In practice, customers must be screened at least against the **EU consolidated sanctions list**, the **UN** lists and the Czech national sanctions list; many exchange offices voluntarily also screen the **OFAC** (US) and **UK** lists. These are not automatically binding in the Czech Republic – they act rather as a risk or contractual factor, particularly in relation to banks and cross-border payments, and their legal effects have to be assessed case by case.

## **When and how often should screening take place?**

Screening must be performed **before executing a transaction** for which you identify the customer, and on an ongoing basis for continuing business relationships (sanctions lists change practically daily). Manual look-ups in public lists are laborious and error-prone – we therefore recommend an **automated solution** that checks the customer's name against current lists in an instant and stores the result for inspection purposes.
